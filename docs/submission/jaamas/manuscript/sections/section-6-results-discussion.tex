\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:6}

This section reports benchmark results on the demo corpus defined in \S\ref{sec:5}, compares ranking methods, interprets outcomes for platform stakeholders, and discusses architectural implications together with measurement limits.
All figures come from committed benchmark artifacts; they do not predict hiring outcomes at production scale.

\subsection{Evaluation scope and protocol}\label{sec:6-evaluated}

We evaluated \emph{resume-to-jobs matching}: for each of 30 sample resumes, the system ranks 15 job postings and we ask how often the right jobs appear near the top.
Human annotators supplied 47 graded pairs---each pair links one resume to one job with a relevance score of none (0), partial (1), or strong (2).
We report results at cutoff $K{=}5$, meaning we judge only the top five jobs shown to a user.

Four questions guided the evaluation.
First, \textbf{precision}: among the five jobs shown, how many are actually relevant?
Second, \textbf{recall}: of all relevant jobs for that person, how many appear in the top five?
Third, \textbf{ranking quality (nDCG@5)}: do \emph{strongly} relevant jobs appear above merely partial ones?
Fourth, \textbf{speed}: how long does each ranking take on this small pool?
Metrics are averaged across all 30 resumes so that no single profile dominates the score.
Formal definitions appear in \S\ref{sec:5}; the same scoring code powers both these offline tests and the live portals (\S\ref{sec:4}).

\subsection{Baseline methods}\label{sec:6-baselines}\label{sec:6-comparison}

We compared methods commonly used in recruitment pipelines, alone and in combination.

\leadpara{Keyword-style methods.}
TF--IDF and BM25 treat resumes and jobs as word lists; they reward shared terms and run very quickly.
Exact skill overlap and Jaccard skill matching reward listed skills without understanding paraphrases.

\leadpara{Meaning-based methods.}
Semantic cosine scoring compares embedding vectors of resume and job text so that related wording can match even when keywords differ.
Multimodal methods blend semantic similarity with skill overlap (default blend: 70\% semantic, 30\% skills).

\leadpara{Combined and advanced methods.}
Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) merges several ranked lists.
Learned fusion trains a small model to weight signals.
Composite scoring---the portal default---adds title fit, experience, compensation, and remote preference on top of semantic and skill channels.
We also tested optional cross-encoder reranking (a slower second-pass scorer) and post-hoc constraint filtering.

Table~\ref{tab:method-comparison} summarizes the head-to-head comparison on the same labeled set.
Multimodal weighted scoring achieves the highest ranking quality (nDCG@5 $= 0.924$) among the methods listed, while BM25 and TF--IDF remain close (nDCG@5 $\approx 0.89$--$0.90$).
On this small job pool, lexical baselines remain competitive, so gains from dense and hybrid matching are measurable but modest.
Semantic similarity alone (nDCG@5 $= 0.878$) underperforms both keyword baselines and multimodal blends, which supports combining \emph{what the text means} with \emph{which skills are named}.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:6-main-result}\label{sec:6-progression}\label{sec:6-fusion}

The progression study (Table~\ref{tab:progression}) ranks every job for every resume and tracks how design choices accumulate.
The best single configuration---multimodal scoring with soft skill embeddings---reaches nDCG@5 $= 0.969$ and recall@5 $= 1.00$, meaning every resume retrieves all of its labeled relevant jobs within the top five.
Plain semantic cosine reaches nDCG@5 $= 0.911$; adding richer text templates nudges it to $0.922$ but still trails the best hybrid.
Merging four lists with RRF ($0.935$) helps diversity but does not beat the strongest single hybrid on this corpus.
Cross-encoder reranking improves over bare semantic search ($0.939$) yet still falls short of soft-embed multimodal ($0.969$) while adding far more compute (Table~\ref{tab:latency}).

\leadpara{Statistical significance.}
Paired bootstrap tests ($N{=}5000$, seed~42) confirm the hybrid gains are not noise: the full composite significantly improves nDCG@5 over semantic-only ranking ($\Delta{=}{+}0.071$, $p{=}0.048$), and multimodal blending and RRF both significantly beat semantic cosine ($p{=}0.010$ and $p{=}0.009$). Lexical baselines (BM25, TF--IDF) do not differ significantly from semantic cosine ($p{>}0.25$), consistent with their close scores on this small pool.

\leadpara{Summary.}
The main finding is modest but consistent: matching improves when the system combines language semantics with explicit skills, and the highest scores arise from hybrid signals rather than either channel alone.
No method achieves high precision---at best, roughly three in ten top-five slots are relevant (P@5 $\approx 0.31$)---because the corpus is small, labels are sparse, and several jobs can appear plausible for the same résumé.
We therefore characterize JobMatch as \emph{assistive} ranking with explainable reasons, not as an automated hiring decision system.

\subsection{Multi-agent state sharing and usability}\label{sec:6-agent-sharing}\label{sec:6-agentic}

The offline tables above test the Matchmaking agent's \emph{scoring} logic.
They do not directly score portal usability or quantify latency perceived by end users.
We separate what the benchmarks show from what the architecture is designed to improve.

When a candidate edits a resume or an employer revises a job description, the owning agent rebuilds a versioned snapshot and shared embedding, then notifies the Matchmaking layer through the event bus (\S\ref{sec:3}--\S\ref{sec:4}).
The event-driven path addresses a usability problem seen in monolithic ATS tools: rankings that stay stale after a profile change, or that change without a visible cause.
Role-separated agents also keep each side's data under its owner's control, which supports trust and auditability even when two users see the same match score from the neutral Matchmaking agent.

State sharing therefore improves \textbf{system usability and traceability}---fresh rankings after edits, clear ownership, structured explanations---more than it maximizes a single offline metric on a frozen corpus.
The benchmarks validate that the shared scoring core ranks sensibly under manual labels; they do not yet prove that multi-agent coordination reduces time-to-hire or increases applicant satisfaction in the field.

\subsection{Strengths}\label{sec:6-strengths}\label{sec:6-ablation-latency}

Several patterns repeat across tables.

\leadpara{Hybrid semantic--skill ranking.}
Any method that combines text meaning with skills beats semantic-only search (nDCG@5 $0.878$ vs.\ $0.917$--$0.949$ for richer composites).
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows that dropping experience, compensation, or location \emph{alone} as the only signal yields very poor rankings (nDCG@5 $\leq 0.39$), but adding them alongside semantic and skill channels contributes to the full composite score ($0.949$ under the portal-default weight set in that table).

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:6-limitations}\label{sec:6-fairness}\label{sec:6-hard-negs}\label{sec:6-latency}

The following limits follow from the corpus size, method choices, and scope of measurement.

\leadpara{Small, controlled dataset.}
Thirty resumes and fifteen jobs make recall@5 easy to saturate---many methods reach ${\ge}0.93$---so differences in nDCG are informative but narrow.
Results may not transfer to employers with thousands of open roles or to domains unlike our demo profiles.

\leadpara{Precision still modest.}
Even the best methods place relevant jobs in only about three of every ten top-five slots (P@5 $\approx 0.31$).
Users will still see imperfect suggestions and must rely on explanations and their own judgment.

\leadpara{Deployment-aligned ablation.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the composite ablation under the portal-default weights (28/27/10/15/10/10, including the title channel), so the offline ablation now matches the deployed scorer; the full composite reaches nDCG@5 $=0.949$, marginally above the earlier research-weight configuration (40/30/15/10/5).

\leadpara{Advanced scorers that disappoint offline.}
Cross-encoder reranking is ${\sim}340\times$ slower than composite bi-encoder scoring on this setup and \emph{lowers} nDCG@5 to $0.834$ (a $0.108$ drop from the bi-encoder baseline) in the committed report---consistent with leaving it disabled by default.
Learned fusion and calibration fit on the same labeled pairs they are judged against, which risks overfitting.

\leadpara{Fairness scope is narrow.}
Table~\ref{tab:fairness} runs a synthetic counterfactual audit on ten fabricated profile pairs (name, summary, or metadata edits).
Seven pairs trigger flags---all with the top-1 job stable, mostly small rank shifts or score changes when non-qualification text changes---with a maximum score shift of $0.017$.
A complementary proxy-group check (\texttt{fairness\_eval.py}) reports selection-rate disparate-impact ratios of $0.82$ across experience tiers and $0.75$ across remote preference (1.0 is parity), committed to \texttt{benchmark\_outputs/fairness\_eval.json}.
This is an engineering sanity check, not demographic fairness certification.

\subsection{Implications for users}\label{sec:6-implications}

\leadpara{Job seekers.}
JobMatch is designed as an assistive representative: maintain a structured profile, inspect why a job ranked highly or poorly, and decide whether to save or apply.
The evaluation indicates that suggestions are most reliable when the system weights both résumé text and listed skills---consistent with how applicants skim postings for fit.
Top-five lists are neither exhaustive nor authoritative; with P@5 near 0.31, several shown jobs may be weak fits, and relevant openings can be missed when the job pool exceeds fifteen roles.

\leadpara{Employers.}
Employers receive symmetric reverse matching: ranked candidates with component-level reasons instead of an opaque score.
Hybrid ranking supports recruiters who weight keywords and hiring managers who read career narrative.
Hard post-filters can remove partially suitable candidates from the graded top five, so the portal keeps constraints optional and off by default.
When a job description changes, the Employer Agent update path refreshes rankings without re-upload to a separate scoring service.

\leadpara{Shared implication.}
Both sides benefit when software explains its suggestions and leaves hire, reject, and apply decisions with users.
The benchmarks support that design on a demo corpus: combined signals rank better than single-signal search, explanations align with composite channels, and the multi-agent layout keeps profile edits on the owning side while a neutral Matchmaking Agent serves both portals.
They do not establish production gains on hiring outcomes; that requires larger labeled datasets, portal-default ablations, and field studies.
