\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

Finding a job today often feels less like a conversation and more like waiting in line without a ticket number.
People send the same resume to many openings, hear nothing for weeks, and cannot tell whether they were overlooked, filtered out, or simply lost in volume.
When something does happen---an interview invite, a rejection, a role that disappears from a results page---the reason rarely travels back to the person who needed it most.

Recruitment platforms were meant to bring order to hiring, yet both sides still describe the same discomfort from different angles.
Candidates face lists that feel arbitrary and feedback that arrives too late to be useful.
Recruiters and hiring managers face tools that move quickly but explain little: promising profiles vanish behind a filter, rankings shift without notice, and no screen makes it obvious how a written requirement relates to a real career path.
Confidence drops when neither party can answer a plain question---\emph{why was this person shown this role, and what would need to change for the answer to differ?}

Those mismatches carry a real price.
Each interview with the wrong person consumes time on both calendars and attention that could have gone elsewhere.
Each strong candidate who never reaches a shortlist can extend a vacancy, force a compromise hire, or send talent to a rival.
Organizations feel the cost in recruiter hours and manager distraction long before salary is discussed; job seekers feel it in repeated preparation for roles that were never a genuine fit.

Sal Khan, in \emph{Brave New Words}, argues that artificial intelligence should work as a personal guide---helping people understand choices, prepare thoughtfully, and move through complex decisions without replacing human judgment.
His discussion of college admissions is especially relevant here: gatekeeping systems often reduce a person to a single snapshot, while a well-designed assistant could track growth, context, and intent over time.
The same shift applies to hiring.
A job seeker deserves software that stands for their goals and history, not only a row in an employer's applicant table.

We build toward that idea with a dedicated representative on each side of the market.
A \textbf{candidate-side agent}---sometimes called a client-side agent in our implementation---accepts resumes and profile updates, turns them into a structured record of skills and experience, and keeps that record current as the person revises their materials.
An \textbf{employer-side agent} performs the parallel work for hiring: it accepts job descriptions and requirements, organizes them into a structured job record, and updates that record when a role changes.
Each agent owns its side of the story; neither is asked to manage the other's data.

Separate ownership only helps if the two sides can still meet on common ground.
When candidate records and job records never connect, both portals collapse into blind browsing.
When one large program controls every edit and every score, users cannot see who changed what or why a recommendation moved.
JobMatch therefore treats \textbf{collaboration among agents} as the core design problem.
Candidate and employer agents maintain their own state; a neutral \textbf{matchmaking agent} reads both, compares them, and returns ranked recommendations together with reasons a human can inspect.
When either side updates a resume or a job description, the owning agent refreshes its record and shares what the matchmaking layer needs so suggestions stay current.

These components form \textbf{JobMatch}, a multi-agentic job-matching system delivered as a research prototype with separate web experiences for candidates, employers, and administrators.
The platform parses resumes and job descriptions into structured states, builds comparable representations of people and roles, and routes matching through the Matchmaking agent to produce ordered recommendations.
For deployment scenarios, the Employer agent can optionally refresh its job corpus from an external provider API; offline evaluation in this paper uses a fixed demo corpus (\S\ref{sec:4}).
Users remain in control throughout: they review extracted fields, read why a pairing ranked as it did, and decide whether to save, apply, or shortlist.
The system advises; it does not hire or reject on anyone's behalf.
We evaluate the matching core offline on a labeled demo corpus---30 resumes, 15 jobs, and 47 graded relevance pairs---using standard quality metrics and reproducible benchmark comparisons reported in later sections.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item A \textbf{candidate/client-side agent} that processes resumes and CVs and maintains candidate state.
  \item An \textbf{employer-side agent} that processes job descriptions and maintains job state.
  \item A \textbf{Matchmaking agent} that uses semantic matching and ranking to produce ordered recommendations with structured explanations.
  \item A \textbf{multi-agent communication flow} in which agents share relevant state and data when profiles change.
  \item A \textbf{complete UI and application layer} demonstrating end-to-end candidate, employer, and admin workflows.
  \item An \textbf{evaluation} using quality metrics and benchmark comparisons on a fixed labeled corpus.
\end{itemize}
